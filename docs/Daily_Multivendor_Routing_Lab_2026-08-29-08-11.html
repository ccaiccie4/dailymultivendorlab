1. Hierarchical eBGP EVPN multihoming

Source: https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst_standalones/Multihoming/multihoming-in-bgp-evpn-vxlan-fabric/hierarchical-multihoming/configure-ebgp-based-evpn-multihoming-and-fabric-network.html

Architecture and intent

Cisco’s hierarchical eBGP EVPN design separates distribution/leaf routing domains from the spine/border layer so a large fabric can scale without forcing every leaf into one flat BGP topology. The spine behaves as a control-plane transit point: it learns EVPN reachability from one block and distributes it to another while policy preserves the originating VTEP as the BGP next hop. The design goal is that the spine does not accidentally become a VXLAN decapsulation/recapsulation waypoint for ordinary east-west traffic.

Control-plane sequence

A leaf originates EVPN NLRI for tenant MAC/IP or prefix reachability. The spine receives the route through eBGP, applies the hierarchical policy, and advertises it onward without changing the VTEP next hop. The receiving leaf imports the route according to RD/RT policy, then performs recursive resolution of the advertised next hop through the underlay. Only after that recursive resolution succeeds can the route be considered usable for forwarding.

Why routes can look healthy while traffic fails

BGP session state and EVPN route presence prove only part of the path. If the retained remote VTEP address is missing from the underlay RIB, resolves through the wrong VRF, or is not programmed into the FIB, the EVPN route can remain visible but unusable. This distinction is especially important in multivendor fabrics because operators often focus on route-target import and overlook the simpler underlay recursion failure.

Verification workflow

Verify the hierarchy in order: eBGP adjacency, received EVPN NLRI, advertised next-hop value, underlay route to that VTEP, FIB/adjacency programming, then VXLAN encapsulation and tenant forwarding. If the first four states are correct but packets still fail, move to VNI/VRF mapping, MTU, BUM handling, and multihoming state instead of continuing to tune BGP policy.

Interoperability and design guidance

Arista, Juniper, FRR and Aruba/HPE all implement EVPN/VXLAN with similar underlay/overlay separation, but their route-server, next-hop, and multichassis defaults can differ. Document whether each eBGP boundary rewrites next hop, which address family carries VTEP reachability, and which loopback is used as the tunnel source. Never assume a vendor’s default next-hop behavior matches Cisco’s hierarchical reference design.

2. All-active EVPN multihoming consistency

Source: https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst_standalones/Multihoming/multihoming-in-bgp-evpn-vxlan-fabric/all-active-mode/restriction-for-aa-multihoming.html

What Cisco is warning about

Cisco’s current all-active EVPN multihoming restrictions are operationally important because an active/active Ethernet Segment is only safe when both VTEPs advertise a consistent representation of the service. The pair must agree on the Ethernet Segment, service/VNI state, and relevant overlay attributes. A mismatch can create a partial control plane in which BGP sessions remain up but remote devices receive an incomplete or contradictory view of the multihomed attachment.

Failure mechanism

Remote VTEPs use EVPN route types and Ethernet Segment information to determine reachability, aliasing, and designated-forwarder behavior. If one local VTEP is missing the EVPN instance, VLAN/VNI mapping, or Ethernet Segment state, some destinations can be attracted to a device that cannot forward them. That produces blackholing that is easy to misread as a generic BGP or LACP problem.

Platform/release caveats

Cisco also documents unsupported combinations and platform restrictions in IOS XE 26.x, including selected Catalyst families, certain combinations with StackWise Virtual, IPv6-only underlay for EVPN multihoming, Layer-2 leaf use in centralized-gateway designs, FHRP on anycast-gateway SVIs, REP/PVLAN on Ethernet Segment ports, and selected access-security features. Those restrictions should be treated as design constraints, not merely troubleshooting footnotes.

Troubleshooting method

Compare both VTEPs side by side. Verify Ethernet Segment identity, EVPN instance/VNI mapping, port-channel/LACP state, local VLAN membership, EVPN advertisements, remote next-hop reachability, and the forwarding result from both chassis. A single-node inspection is insufficient in an active/active design because the defect may be asymmetry between peers.

Prevention

Use a pair-consistency checklist in provisioning and upgrades. Validate both the configured state and the operational state before introducing endpoints, and repeat the comparison after software upgrades or service changes. This practice generalizes directly to Arista MLAG/EVPN and Aruba VSX/EVPN even though the proprietary synchronization mechanisms differ.

3. Routed overlay and IRB mapping boundaries

Source: https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/switches/lan/catalyst_standalones/Multihoming/multihoming-in-bgp-evpn-vxlan-fabric/routed-overlay-mh/routed-overlay-network-reference-configuration.html

Routed-overlay mental model

The routed-overlay reference design combines host-facing redundancy, a Layer-3 underlay, and EVPN/VXLAN tenant services. Those layers must be tested independently. A healthy access EtherChannel or Ethernet Segment proves only that the endpoint has redundant attachment; it does not prove tenant route import, remote VTEP recursion, or overlay forwarding.

Mapping boundaries that commonly fail

Treat VLAN↔EVI/VNI, SVI↔VRF, VRF↔route-target, EVPN route↔next-hop, and endpoint MAC/IP↔local attachment as explicit state boundaries. A single mismatch can produce a fabric that is almost correct: routes may exist, tunnels may be up, and the local gateway may respond, while one tenant subnet still blackholes.

Control/data-plane sequence

A host resolves its first-hop gateway locally. The VTEP performs bridging or IRB according to the destination, selects tenant reachability from the VRF, resolves the remote VTEP in the underlay, encapsulates into VXLAN, and sends the outer IP packet across the fabric. The receiving VTEP decapsulates and performs local bridging/routing. Every step is independently verifiable.

Troubleshooting

Start at the endpoint and stop at the first missing state: local VLAN and gateway/ARP, local VRF route, EVPN route import, underlay VTEP recursion, VXLAN encapsulation, remote endpoint learning. This method prevents the common habit of changing BGP policy when the real failure is a local VLAN/VNI mismatch.

Design guidance

Stretch only the services that require it, keep tenant and underlay routing tables clearly separated, and document each mapping. In multivendor environments, normalize terminology—VNI, EVI, bridge-domain, routing-instance/VRF—so the engineering team compares equivalent objects rather than command names.

4. BGP multipath before SD-WAN steering

Source: https://community.fortinet.com/fortigate-3/technical-tip-configuring-sd-wan-traffic-steering-with-bgp-learned-routes-229549

What the Fortinet example proves

Fortinet’s August 24, 2026 technical tip demonstrates a subtle but critical distinction: two eBGP sessions can both be Established and the BGP table can contain two valid paths while the routing table installs only one best path. SD-WAN cannot steer over a BGP path that the routing process has not installed as a usable next hop.

Control-plane sequence

The local FortiGate learns 192.168.2.0/24 over two eBGP peers. Before multipath, both paths are visible in BGP but only one becomes active in the RIB. Enabling `set ebgp-multipath enable` allows both otherwise-equal paths to be installed. SD-WAN then has two real forwarding candidates instead of merely two BGP advertisements.

Verification chain

The source deliberately verifies several state boundaries: `get router info bgp summary` confirms peer state; `get router info bgp network <prefix>` shows candidate BGP paths; the routing-table command proves which next hops are installed; `diagnose sys sdwan service` shows member eligibility; and session output proves the actual gateway/member used by a real flow. The last step matters because policy intent is not proof of forwarding.

Failure diagnosis

If the second peer is Established but only one next hop is active, changing SD-WAN priority is premature. Compare BGP attributes and multipath eligibility first. If both next hops are installed but only one SD-WAN member is eligible, move to SD-WAN service, health-check, and policy state.

Interoperability

The same principle applies when the upstream peers are Cisco, Arista, Juniper, or FRR. Control-plane diversity becomes useful to FortiGate SD-WAN only after FortiOS has selected and installed multiple forwarding paths.

5. Performance SLA when BGP remains Established

Source: https://community.fortinet.com/fortigate-3/technical-tip-using-sd-wan-performance-sla-to-steer-traffic-when-bgp-remains-established-229544

Why BGP Established is insufficient

Fortinet’s companion August 24 article shows a failure beyond the BGP peer while the BGP TCP session and learned routes remain healthy. BGP proves reachability between the two BGP endpoints and continued route exchange; it does not prove that an application destination behind the peer remains reachable.

SLA design

Fortinet configures an SD-WAN Performance SLA toward a target inside the remote service path rather than merely pinging the BGP neighbor. Both SD-WAN members are monitored, with explicit source addresses so each probe tests the intended path. A valid return path to those source addresses is required.

Failure sequence

When reachability through one member is intentionally broken beyond the BGP peer, `diagnose sys sdwan health-check` marks that member dead while both BGP sessions remain Established and both BGP next hops remain in the routing table. `diagnose sys sdwan service` then removes the unhealthy member from selection without requiring BGP withdrawal.

Packet proof

Session inspection shows the selected gateway and fields such as `sdwan_mbr_seq` and `sdwan_service_id`. Those fields prove that an actual flow followed the surviving path. This is stronger evidence than simply reading the configured service priority.

Design lesson

Use the monitoring target that represents the application reachability you actually care about. Monitoring only the BGP peer detects peer failure but can miss failures beyond it. This is a clean example of control-plane health versus service-path health.

6. OSPF tags redistributed into BGP

Source: https://community.fortinet.com/fortigate-3/technical-tip-wrong-route-tag-added-to-bgp-routes-redistributed-from-ospf-214352

Why tags matter

Route tags are frequently used to preserve route provenance across redistribution boundaries. A tag can identify “originally OSPF,” “originally BGP,” or a particular domain so the return boundary can reject the route and prevent feedback loops. That makes the tag part of the correctness of the design, not decorative metadata.

Documented FortiOS issue

Fortinet documents affected releases where very high OSPF tag values can be represented incorrectly after redistribution into BGP. A policy that expects an exact tag can therefore stop matching after protocol conversion.

Operational consequence

If a mutated tag is used for loop prevention, the route can be re-advertised into its source domain or selected by the wrong policy. This can create intermittent preference changes or genuine loops depending on the surrounding topology and administrative-distance relationships.

Verification

Check the tag in the OSPF LSDB before redistribution, inspect the route-map that matches/sets metadata, then inspect the BGP route after redistribution. Do not assume the value survived simply because both protocols support a tag/community-like field.

Prevention

Use conservative tag ranges on affected code, include tag preservation in upgrade regression tests, and favor explicit deny-on-return policy. In multivendor designs, document numeric ranges that are known to survive each vendor’s conversion path.

7. Advanced Routing Engine filter attachment points

Source: https://docs.paloaltonetworks.com/ngfw/networking/advanced-routing/create-filters-for-the-advanced-routing-engine

Policy toolkit

PAN-OS Advanced Routing Engine provides access lists, prefix lists, redistribution route maps, BGP route maps, AS-path access lists, and community lists. The important point is not only what each object can match but where that object is referenced.

Attachment-point semantics

A prefix list may participate in route acceptance into the local RIB, redistribution between protocols, advertisement to a peer, conditional advertisement, aggregation, or attribute manipulation. The same prefix match therefore has different operational meaning depending on the stage.

Sequential evaluation

Palo Alto documents sequential evaluation for prefix lists and route-map entries. Once an entry matches, the permit/deny or set behavior applies according to that policy object. Order and scope are therefore part of the policy, not just the individual match terms.

Troubleshooting workflow

Ask where the route first disappears: source RIB, redistribution candidate set, destination protocol RIB, or neighbor advertisement. Inspect the filter only at that first failing stage. If the route never entered BGP, changing outbound BGP filtering cannot fix it; if it exists in BGP but is absent from one peer, redistribution is probably not the boundary.

Cross-vendor translation

When translating a Cisco/Fortinet/Arista route-map to PAN-OS, first record the business intent and pipeline stage. Only then map match/set constructs. Command-name similarity is not semantic equivalence.

8. OSPF migration exceptions

Source: https://docs.paloaltonetworks.com/pan-os/u-v/routing-engine-migration-reference/routing-protocol-migration-exceptions/ospf

Migration risk

PAN-OS documents migration exceptions between legacy routing behavior and the Advanced Routing Engine. OSPF export policy in the legacy model can match dimensions such as path type, area, next hop, interface, prefix, and tag; the newer redistribution model does not always expose a one-for-one equivalent for every legacy path-type/area condition.

Why a clean commit can still be wrong

A configuration can migrate without syntax errors but broaden or narrow the candidate set. A rule that intended “only these E1 routes from this area” can become a more generic prefix/tag policy unless the original intent is explicitly reconstructed.

Pre-migration inventory

Build a route-policy matrix before the change: source protocol, area, path type, prefix, tag, expected redistribution result, expected BGP advertisement, and expected winning RIB route. Include routes that must remain denied.

Validation

After migration, inspect the source OSPF RIB/LSDB, redistribution match result, BGP RIB, and peer advertisement. Test intra-area, inter-area, E1, E2, NSSA, and tagged routes where the production policy differentiates them.

Design lesson

Tags can become a durable cross-version/cross-vendor provenance marker when vendor-specific path-type matching differs, but only if the tag is set consistently and verified after conversion.

9. Redistribution versus BGP export

Source: https://docs.paloaltonetworks.com/ngfw/networking/configure-route-redistribution

Two distinct questions

Redistribution asks: “Should this route enter another routing protocol?” BGP export asks: “Should this BGP route be advertised to this peer or peer group?” Those are different policy boundaries and should not be collapsed into one mental model.

Route lifecycle

A connected/static/OSPF route first exists in the source routing table, then matches a redistribution profile or route map, then appears in the local BGP RIB, then may be filtered or modified by peer export policy. Each stage can independently permit, deny, or alter the route.

Failure examples

If the route is missing from the local BGP RIB, troubleshoot redistribution. If it is present locally but absent from one neighbor, troubleshoot export. If it is present at the peer but not selected, troubleshoot the peer’s own best-path process and next-hop reachability.

Operational safety

Use the narrowest policy stage that satisfies the requirement. A peer-specific exception should usually not be implemented by globally changing redistribution because that can affect every BGP peer.

Interoperability

This lifecycle maps cleanly to Cisco/Arista/FRR route-maps and FortiGate redistribution plus neighbor policy. Normalize the stage first, then syntax.

10. EVPN Type-5 DCI re-origination

Source: https://www.arista.com/en/um-eos/eos-configuring-evpn

Why a DCI gateway is a redistribution boundary

Arista’s EVPN Type-5 multi-domain design allows a gateway to terminate one EVPN domain and re-originate IP-prefix reachability into another. The route is no longer merely reflected; it is represented in a new domain context with different RD/RT policy.

Route identity and policy

RDs keep otherwise-identical VPN routes unique, while RT import/export controls which VRFs accept them. At a DCI boundary, the gateway must map the source domain’s route into the destination domain without leaking internal topology or allowing the route to circulate back indefinitely.

Loop prevention

Re-origination creates a problem analogous to redistribution loops. Arista documents domain/path mechanisms such as D-path handling so a route that already traversed a domain boundary can be identified and rejected if it returns through another gateway.

Troubleshooting

Inspect the Type-5 route on both sides of the gateway: RD, RT, next hop, domain/path metadata, VRF import, and recursive next-hop resolution. A route can be valid in one domain and absent in another because of intentional RT policy.

Design guidance

Treat DCI gateways as policy boundaries. Document which prefixes cross, how they are summarized, what provenance metadata is carried, and how alternate gateways prevent re-entry.

11. Single-gateway centralized routing and BUM

Source: https://www.arista.com/en/um-eos/eos-evpn-vxlan-single-gateway-centralized-routing

Traditional centralized-gateway model

In centralized routing, a small set of L3 VTEPs performs inter-subnet routing for the fabric. Redundant gateway designs can advertise both physical and virtual VTEP identities so endpoint gateway state remains stable during node failure.

Why BUM can be duplicated

If a topology really has only one centralized gateway or one MLAG pair, advertising multiple equivalent VTEP identities into the EVPN floodset can cause the same broadcast/unknown-unicast/multicast traffic to be replicated unnecessarily.

Control plane can be correct but inefficient

Type-3/IMET membership can be completely valid while the data plane sends more copies than required. This is not reachability failure; it is a scaling and bandwidth-efficiency problem.

Troubleshooting

When BUM traffic is unexpectedly high, inspect flood-list membership, VTEP identities, gateway mode, and whether the design actually needs both virtual and physical replication targets. Compare packet counters rather than relying only on BGP route counts.

Design lesson

BUM behavior is one reason to minimize unnecessary Layer-2 extension. A working EVPN control plane does not guarantee an efficient replication topology.

12. Control plane versus data plane

Source: https://www.arista.com/en/um-eos/eos-data-transfer

EOS forwarding model

Arista explicitly separates routing control-plane computation from hardware Layer-3 switching. Protocols populate routing state; the system then programs forwarding information into hardware-optimized data structures.

Why this matters in troubleshooting

A route in BGP or even the RIB does not by itself prove the ASIC has a complete, usable adjacency. Next-hop recursion, interface state, ARP/ND, MTU, ACL/policy, or hardware programming can still prevent successful forwarding.

Evidence chain

Verify protocol adjacency and candidate route, then active RIB selection, next-hop resolution, forwarding adjacency, interface/error counters, and packet observation. This sequence prevents repeated control-plane tuning after the route-selection problem has already been solved.

Redistribution connection

Only routes represented in the local routing system become candidates for redistribution according to configured policy. Route origin, active status, and preference can therefore change which routes leave the box.

Universal lesson

RIB/FIB separation is one of the most portable multivendor troubleshooting concepts. It lets engineers compare behavior without getting trapped by vendor-specific CLI vocabulary.

show interfaces counters errors
Inspect forwarding/adjacency state

13. VSX logical VTEP with independent BGP speakers

Source: https://www.arubanetworks.com/techdocs/AOS-CX/10.15/PDF/vsx.pdf

VSX dual identity

AOS-CX VSX peers can be independent BGP speakers toward the fabric while presenting a common logical VTEP address for VXLAN forwarding. Separate control-plane addresses establish peerings; the shared VTEP identity gives remote devices one stable overlay next hop.

Failure implication

One peer’s BGP uplink can fail while the shared VTEP remains reachable through the surviving peer. Conversely, both BGP sessions can be healthy while host-facing VSX state, ISL behavior, or the logical VTEP data path is impaired.

Underlay dependency

Remote VTEPs still need an underlay route to the common VTEP address. EVPN does not create reachability to its own next hop. If the logical VTEP address is not recursively resolvable, imported EVPN routes cannot forward.

Troubleshooting

Check VSX peer/ISL state, both BGP EVPN sessions, the route to the logical VTEP, VXLAN tunnel state, endpoint MAC/ARP/ND, and actual packet forwarding. Compare both chassis rather than treating the pair as a single debugging object.

Interoperability

Cisco, Arista, Juniper, or FRR remote VTEPs do not need to understand VSX internals. Their interoperability contract is standards-based EVPN information plus IP reachability to the advertised VTEP next hop.

14. OSPF redistribution and active-routes-only

Source: https://www.arubanetworks.com/techdocs/AOS-CX/10.15/PDF/ip_route_6300-6400-8100-83xx-9300-10000.pdf

Redistribution source set

AOS-CX OSPF can redistribute routes from sources such as BGP, connected, static, RIP, and other routing processes, optionally controlled by route maps. The operator must distinguish a route that exists in a protocol database from one that is eligible to be redistributed.

Active-routes-only behavior

AOS-CX documents `route-redistribute active-routes-only`, which restricts candidates to routes selected as active for forwarding. If the same prefix is learned from BGP and static, only the winning route may become a redistribution candidate depending on the feature state.

Failure diagnosis

A route can be visible under BGP yet absent from OSPF externals because it lost RIB selection—not because the route map denied it. Check active RIB state before debugging the redistribution policy.

OSPF output

After candidate selection and route-map evaluation, verify the resulting external LSA and how it propagates through area types. Confirm metric and metric type as well as prefix presence.

Cross-vendor lesson

Some platforms redistribute from a protocol’s known routes while others effectively operate from active RIB candidates by default or by option. Record that behavior explicitly when migrating designs.

15. Layered VXLAN troubleshooting with VSX

Source: https://www.arubanetworks.com/techdocs/AOS-CX/10.15/PDF/vsx.pdf

Why layered troubleshooting fits VSX EVPN

A VSX EVPN fabric naturally contains several independent layers: host-facing multichassis attachment, underlay IP reachability, two BGP EVPN speakers, a shared VTEP identity, tenant VNI/VRF state, and endpoint learning.

Recommended order

Start with physical/VSX/LACP state, then prove underlay route to the logical VTEP, then BGP EVPN adjacency and route import, then VNI/VRF mapping, then endpoint MAC/ARP/ND, then actual packet forwarding. This order follows dependencies rather than vendor menus.

Common misdiagnosis

If the logical VTEP route is missing, spending time on EVPN RT policy is premature. If the underlay and EVPN routes are correct but one dual-homed server fails, move down to VSX/LACP/VLAN state.

Multivendor use

The same sequence works when the remote fabric is Cisco or Arista because the dependency graph is standards-based: IP reachability first, EVPN control plane next, VXLAN data plane after that.

Lab method

Introduce one failure at each layer and record which show command changes first. Over time this builds a symptom-to-boundary map that is more valuable than memorizing isolated commands.

16. EVPN-VXLAN with IPv6 underlay

Source: https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/evpn/topics/topic-map/vxlan-ipv6-underlay-overview.html

IPv6 underlay model

Juniper documents EVPN-VXLAN fabrics where VTEP reachability uses IPv6 rather than IPv4. The overlay still uses EVPN route types and VXLAN; the outer tunnel header and underlay routing state change address family.

Supported underlay protocols and route types

Juniper qualifies iBGP/eBGP and OSPFv3 for the IPv6 underlay on supported platforms. Type 1/2/3/4 routes are broadly supported, with Type-5 support on specified platform families. Platform and release matrices matter because optional feature combinations are not universal.

Important limitations

The documentation warns that IPv4 and IPv6 underlays cannot simply be mixed across EVPN instances in one fabric, and IPv6-underlay support is tied to MAC-VRF/VXLAN designs on supported systems. Some platforms require system-profile or tunnel-termination specifics.

Control/data-plane consequence

A remote EVPN route can be correct while the IPv6 route to the remote VTEP is missing. Troubleshooting therefore shifts recursive lookups and neighbor/forwarding evidence into the IPv6 underlay without changing the basic overlay mental model.

MTU and migration

The outer IPv6 header changes encapsulation overhead compared with IPv4. Validate MTU end-to-end. When migrating an existing IPv4 underlay, remove incompatible underlay-specific configuration and follow platform reboot/profile requirements rather than treating the change as an address-only edit.

17. Routing protocols on EVPN IRB

Source: https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/evpn/topics/concept/protocols-evpn-vxlan.html

IRB as a routing-protocol boundary

Junos supports OSPF, IS-IS, and BGP adjacencies on IRB interfaces in EVPN-VXLAN designs. That means an IRB can serve both as a first-hop gateway for endpoints and as a routed handoff toward a firewall, load balancer, CE, or other service device.

Two independent control planes

The local OSPF/BGP/IS-IS adjacency on the IRB is separate from the EVPN overlay. An IRB neighbor can be healthy while remote VNI import is broken, or EVPN can be healthy while the attached routing neighbor is down because of BFD, timers, authentication, or interface policy.

Troubleshooting

Prove local IRB interface state and routing adjacency, local RIB installation, redistribution/advertisement into EVPN if applicable, remote route import, and VTEP recursion. Avoid assuming an adjacency problem merely because the end-to-end tenant path fails.

Integration use case

This is a common place to connect Palo Alto or FortiGate appliances to a fabric. The firewall may run eBGP/OSPF to the IRB while the switching fabric uses EVPN internally.

Design caution

Be explicit about where route ownership changes. If the IRB learns external routes and also participates in overlay routing, apply filtering and provenance controls to prevent accidental feedback between the attached protocol and EVPN.

18. Type-5 subnet routes for scale

Source: https://www.juniper.net/documentation/us/en/software/junos/release-notes/25.4/junos-release-notes-25.4r1/topics/new-features/feature-descriptions/evpn-9.html

Scale problem

Large stretched VXLAN fabrics can create excessive host-specific routing state if every endpoint prefix is propagated to every tier. Juniper’s newer Type-5 capabilities and policy options let designers keep host detail where it is operationally useful while advertising subnet-level reachability to other layers.

Abstraction boundary

A Type-5 subnet route tells a receiving device how to reach a prefix without requiring one route per host. This reduces BGP state, RIB/FIB entries, update churn, and convergence work on tiers that do not need endpoint granularity.

Troubleshooting tradeoff

With summarization or subnet-only advertisement, an individual host outage does not necessarily correspond to a missing route at every layer. The edge may retain the subnet route while the endpoint-specific state is missing closer to the access layer.

Design method

Document which tier owns host specificity, which tier advertises subnet reachability, and what failure visibility is lost by aggregation. Make monitoring aware of that distinction.

Cross-vendor lesson

Cisco and Arista fabrics face the same scale tradeoff even when the exact policy knob differs. Route granularity is an architecture decision, not merely a BGP optimization.

19. EVPN depends on Linux bridge/VRF state

Source: https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/evpn.html

FRR architecture

FRRouting provides the EVPN control plane but commonly relies on Linux kernel objects for the data plane. L2VNIs map to MAC-VRF/bridge/VXLAN constructs; L3VNIs map to IP-VRF relationships. Correct BGP EVPN state therefore depends on correct Linux bridge, SVI, VRF, FDB, neighbor, and route state.

Why this is different operationally

On an integrated switch NOS, many of those objects are managed behind one CLI. With FRR, it is common to inspect both FRR and native Linux commands. A route can exist in `show bgp l2vpn evpn` while the kernel bridge or VRF association is wrong.

Scale caveat

FRR documentation calls out Linux neighbor-cache considerations for larger EVPN host counts. A control plane can scale logically while the host OS begins aggressive garbage collection or neighbor-table exhaustion.

Troubleshooting

Inspect EVPN routes, Linux links, bridge FDB, neighbor table, VRF routes, and VXLAN devices together. The first inconsistent object is usually more valuable than another BGP reset.

Interoperability

Remote hardware VTEPs see normal EVPN/VXLAN behavior and cannot diagnose the Linux-local failure. That is why multivendor incident response must include platform-local data-plane evidence.

20. OSPF redistribution by area type

Source: https://docs.frrouting.org/en/latest/ospfd.html

Redistribution into OSPF

FRR OSPF supports redistribution from several routing sources and can apply route maps, metric values, and E1/E2 metric type. The resulting external route representation depends on OSPF area type as well as redistribution policy.

Area-type behavior

Normal external-capable areas carry Type-5 LSAs. NSSAs use Type-7 LSAs for locally introduced externals, which can be translated at the NSSA boundary. Stub areas intentionally do not receive ordinary external LSAs. An absent route in a stub area may therefore be correct design behavior.

Troubleshooting

Prove the source route, route-map match, generated external LSA type, ABR behavior, and receiving-area policy. Do not treat every missing external as a redistribution failure.

Metric semantics

Choose E1/E2 intentionally. E1 adds internal cost toward the ASBR, while E2 typically keeps the external metric dominant; the choice affects path selection in multi-exit designs.

Cross-vendor lesson

These are protocol-standard OSPF concepts and should be recognizable across Cisco, Juniper, Arista, Aruba, and Fortinet even though command syntax differs.

21. IS-IS Segment Routing capability matrix

Source: https://docs.frrouting.org/en/stable-10.0/isisd.html

FRR Segment Routing model

FRR documents IS-IS Segment Routing for the MPLS data plane, including Prefix-SID advertisement, SRGB configuration, and node MSD. This allows FRR to participate in standards-based SR labs with other routers.

Capability matrix matters

The documented version also lists limitations such as missing optional capabilities or restricted algorithms/label blocks. A device saying “Segment Routing supported” does not imply parity with every feature available on Cisco or Juniper.

Control/data-plane sequence

IS-IS advertises SID information in TLVs, the local routing process selects paths, the system allocates/programs MPLS labels, and packets follow the label stack. Troubleshooting must therefore prove both IS-IS advertisement and MPLS forwarding state.

Interoperability testing

Compare Prefix-SID advertisement, SRGB assumptions, label values, node MSD, and supported algorithms across FRR and peer vendors. Test failure and reconvergence rather than validating only a steady-state ping.

Design guidance

Build a feature matrix before production: adjacency, SR-MPLS data plane, Prefix-SID, SRGB/SRLB, TI-LFA or algorithm support, binding SID, SRMS, and level behavior. Standards compliance is the starting point; optional feature parity determines deployability.
